## 0. What this is, and the two questions it answers

Access control in a claim system is two questions, and specifications routinely answer only the first:

1. **May this role perform this function?** — the familiar one.
2. **On whose data?** — the one that gets missed, and the one that matters. An assessor may assess claims; may they assess *any* claim, including their own family's? (**No — §4.12.**) A customer may view claims; whose? (**Only those they submitted — §4.3.**)

This annex answers both, for all **53** features (Framework v2.5 §7.1). **A permission without a data scope is not a permission** — it is a function name with a tick beside it, and it is how systems end up letting a user read a record they should never see.

**It also answers a third question from v1.1, and answering it is what makes the first two honest: *is this ours to grant at all?*** Of the 53 features, **38 are ours** — Group C (10), Group E (20), Group F (4) and **Group G (4, new at v1.4)** — and every one of them carries a grant this matrix enforces at our API. **The arithmetic is corrected here as well as extended**: v1.3 said *33 of 48* with Group E at 19, which was one behind from the moment F60 was added, and Framework v2.4 §7.1 fixed the same sum from its own side. **Framework v2.5 changes no count** — **C-BUS-12** would add an **F69** for the adjudicable benefit configuration under one of its two answers, and no grant is pre-made against a feature that may not exist (§8). The other **15 are not**: Group A (7) is InsureMO's and Group D (8) is IDP's, and their functions sit behind another party's login. They keep their rows, and the rows say *not ours to grant* rather than being quietly dropped. Three of the fifteen are exceptions worth naming — **F01**, **F36** and **F38** — and each is called out where it appears.

### 0.1 How to read the matrix

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **F** | Full — may perform the function without restriction within the stated data scope |
| **R** | Read only |
| **C** | Conditional — permitted, subject to a named rule in §4 |
| **—** | Not permitted. The function is not visible, not merely disabled |

> **"Not visible" is deliberate.** A disabled button tells a user the capability exists and invites them to ask why they cannot use it. In MVP 1 an unavailable function is absent from the interface, and the API rejects it independently — the UI is a convenience, never the control.

### 0.2 The four roles

Per A1 §7. Four roles is a deliberate simplification: the reference systems carry a multi-level authority matrix with cumulative-across-claim-case logic (Framework §7.4, S-06), and excluding it is what makes a two-journey MVP possible.

| Role | Identifier | Interface | Population |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer | `CUSTOMER` | Mobile app only | Policyholders and insured members |
| Assessor | `ASSESSOR` | Web console | Claim handling staff |
| Approver | `APPROVER` | Web console | Staff with settlement authority |
| Administrator | `ADMIN` | Web console | System and master-data administration |

#### 0.2a Two grants that are not roles — `TERM_AUTHOR` and `TERM_APPROVE`

**New at v1.4, and the shape matters more than the names.** The term layer needs two authorities that no existing role should acquire by accident, so they are **named grants held alongside a role**, exactly as the `clinical` grant is in §4.13 — not a fifth and sixth role, and not an implication of `ADMIN`.

| Grant | Held by | Confers |
|---|---|---|
| **`TERM_AUTHOR`** | A named user, whatever role they hold | Create and edit a term in `DRAFT`; submit for approval. **Never approval** — §4.24 |
| **`TERM_APPROVE`** | A **different** named user | Approve, return to draft, withdraw. **Never authorship of the version they approve** — §4.25 |

> **Why not simply give it to `ADMIN`.** An administrator maintains configuration; a term is **contractual text that decides whether a customer is paid**. §4.11 already keeps `ADMIN` out of every claim decision, and a term is a claim decision written in advance. **Granting authorship with `ADMIN` would let one account both write a term and hold every other administrative right in the system**, which is §4.8's escalation problem with a contract attached. D2 §5.28 states the same thing from the screen's side: *"`ADMIN` is not granted authorship by holding `ADMIN`."*
>
> **A user may hold both grants** — the pilot team is small — **and §4.25 then binds per term version**, exactly as §4.1 binds per claim. Holding both is permitted; using both on one term version is not.

**A user may hold more than one role**, and in a small pilot team that is likely — but §4.1's segregation rule then binds *per claim*, not per user. Holding both `ASSESSOR` and `APPROVER` is permitted; using both on the same claim is not.

> **Confirmed 17/08/2026 (D1 B-07): a deliberate two-level model — submit and approve.** The pilot team is small, all four roles are filled, and there is no authority hierarchy above the single approval step. This *confirms* declared simplification S-12 rather than straining it, and §4.1 remains workable precisely because it binds per claim.
>
> **Two consequences to note.** **§4.7's second-administrator rule is live**, because auto-approval is in MVP 1 (B-12, revised) and the threshold is the number that decides how much money moves without a human. **§4.9's second-administrator rule on import commit also stands** — now over **F59**, the group bulk load, which is ours. These are the only two places the two-level model is exceeded on purpose, and both guard an action with a blast radius far wider than one claim.

### 0.3 Data scopes

| Scope | Definition |
|---|---|
| `SELF` | Records where the user is the policyholder or an insured member on the policy |
| `ASSIGNED` | Claims assigned to this user |
| `QUEUE` | Claims in a work queue the user is a member of — assigned or unassigned. **In MVP 1 there is exactly one organisational unit** (decision B3, Framework C-BUS-10), so the unit half of this predicate resolves to *everything* and the scope reduces to the queue. **It is specified as a predicate anyway** — but see the note below, because a control that cannot fail is worse than no control |
| `ALL_CLAIMS` | Every claim in the system |
| `ALL_POLICIES` | Every policy and member record **that InsureMO will answer for**. Distinct from `ALL_CLAIMS`: policy data carries no clinical detail, so read access to it is a materially smaller grant. **Redefined at v1.1** — it used to range over a table we held. It now bounds a **read of a vendor through I-05**, exercised one policy or one search at a time, and it confers **no write of any kind** (§4.15). The scope name is kept because it still answers *on whose data*, which is the question §0 says matters |
| `MASTER` | Configuration and master data. **Contains no personal data — and from v1.5 that sentence is load-bearing rather than descriptive.** D2 `V-759` found two term-register screens rendering **claim numbers** on this scope, and a claim number is not master data: it resolves to one person's medical claim and it is the key every other console screen opens a clinical record with. **§4.29 settles what those screens may show** |
| `NONE` | No data access; the function does not exist for this role |

### 3.2 Policy and member data, the payee, and the group load

#### 3.2.1 The read grants survive — as reads of InsureMO through I-05

**Two things this table says that a reader should not have to infer.**

**`ADMIN` is `—` on every row that writes, and `R` on the one that reads** — corrected at v1.5, and the correction is narrower than it looks. An administrator maintains reference data; **a term is contractual text that decides whether a customer is paid**, and §4.11 already keeps `ADMIN` out of claim decisions. A term is a claim decision written in advance. **`ADMIN` does not become a term author by being an administrator** (§0.2a), and nothing on the authoring, approval, back-dating or correction rows has changed.

**What changed is the read, and the reason is a silent failure rather than a convenience.** §3.1 gives `ADMIN` the adjudicable benefit configuration and the service-category master. **A term's scope is a benefit code**, and D2 §5.28.7 specifies what happens when that code no longer resolves: **the term stops being evaluated.** Not an error, not a checkpoint — it stops. **So an administrator holding `—` on every term row could rename a benefit code, silently disable an `ACTIVE` contractual exclusion, and have had no way of finding out that any term referenced it.** The two available fixes were *let the administrator see the terms* and *block the benefit edit while a term references it*. **The read is chosen** — §4.30 — because it is one query rather than a referential-integrity check across two masters, and because **it prevents the mistake rather than reporting it after the edit is refused.** It confers nothing else: the register carries no personal data, and reading a clause is not reading a claim.

**`ASSESSOR` and `APPROVER` read the register and cannot write it.** They must be able to open the clause a checkpoint quotes and the clause a decline cites, **at the version the claim was evaluated against and not the version in force today** (D2 `V-740`). That is a read, and it is the same posture as §3.2.1's read of policy data: **the person assessing the claim must not be able to change what the contract is taken to say.**

| **§4.24** | **`TERM_AUTHOR` — authoring a term.** The holder may **create a term and edit it while it is `DRAFT`**, and may submit it for approval. **They may not approve it, they may not approve any other version they authored, and they may not withdraw an `ACTIVE` term.** A draft is **invisible to the engine** (D2 `V-735`), so a half-written selector can never decide a claim; every save is audited field by field (F63); and the platform **refuses an incomplete save rather than completing it** — a system that supplied a missing `selector` would be authoring a contractual term. **The grant is over the register, not over a claim**, so its scope is `MASTER` and it confers no claim access of any kind | **New at v1.4, closing `D2-175`.** The `DRAFT` boundary is what makes the grant safe to give: an author can be wrong all day and no claim is affected, because the engine evaluates only `ACTIVE` terms inside their effective window. **What an author cannot be is alone** — §4.25 |
| **§4.25** | **`TERM_APPROVE` — four eyes, and it is an enforced rule rather than a convention.** The holder may move a term version to `PENDING_ACTIVE` and `ACTIVE`, return it to `DRAFT` with a reason, and **withdraw** an `ACTIVE` term. **The author and the approver must be different people. So on the `ASSIGNED` scope the queue was empty for every user until somebody had already picked the claim up from the work list, at which point the queue is telling them something they are looking at. **A queue that only shows work you have already found is not a queue.** D2 §5.28.5's correction routing failed identically and for the same reason: it delivers *"to a named human queue"*, and the queue had no members. **`QUEUE` is the right scope and it is the one S-01 already uses** — one organisational unit, so it resolves to *everything* (see the note at §7), which is honest rather than tidy. **The outcome stays at `ASSIGNED` and that half is unchanged: an uphold is a decline**, so it sits with whoever already holds decline authority on that line — the assessor, per F46 and F47, and not a new population. Granting an uphold to anyone who cannot already decline a line would create a second route to a decline with a different control around it. **`ADMIN` is excluded on §4.11's own reasoning and it is stated rather than inferred**: a checkpoint carries the clause, the claim, the facts the selector matched on and a clinical judgement, and an administrator has no business in any of them. **A clear needs a reason for the opposite reason to an uphold** — it produces nothing the customer ever sees, so its whole audience is the auditor asking why a contractual term was not applied |
| **§4.27** | **Back-dating an effective date past the earliest open claim requires an explicit recorded override, and only `TERM_APPROVE` may grant it.** Where a term's `effectiveFrom` is earlier than the oldest non-terminal claim bound to that rule set, the platform **refuses the save and names the claim number and the date that blocked it** (D2 `V-737`). | **§4.29** | **A term-register screen shows counts and dates. It does not show claim numbers, and no reveal control is built.** `TERM_AUTHOR` and `TERM_APPROVE` hold data scope **`MASTER`**. §4.27's back-dating refusal renders **how many** open claims block the date and **the earliest** of their registration dates; §4.28's correction impact analysis renders **how many** in-flight claims are bound to the rule set, **how many** would change result, and **how many** are excluded as `PAID` or `CLOSED`. **The claim numbers themselves are not rendered to a `MASTER` scope, by any control, to any grant** | **This decides D2 `D2-182`, and it takes the narrower of the two answers D2 offered.** D2 `V-759` proposed the numbers behind an explicit, logged control available only to a user who also holds a claims scope, and asked A3 whether `TERM_AUTHOR` should instead gain a narrow claim-reference read. **Neither is granted.** The reasoning is §0's: **a permission without a data scope is not a permission**, and a control that renders claim-scoped data on a `MASTER`-scoped screen is a scope violation whether or not the acting user happens to hold a second grant that would have permitted it elsewhere. **Enforcement lives at the query layer** (§6), where the scope is a predicate; #### 4.31a The roster table

**Read it as a floor, not a target.** Every cell says *a different person from*, and nothing here forbids one person holding several grants — §0.2 permits it and §4.1, §4.25 and §4.7 all bind per object rather than per person.

| Grant / duty | Held by at least | Must differ from | Why the pair cannot collapse |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ASSESSOR` | **2** | — | Not a rule but a consequence: with one assessor, **§4.12** (conflict of interest) has no relief valve — a claim naming that person as insured, claimant or beneficiary has nobody to go to, and the only exit is `CANCELLED` |
| `APPROVER` | **1**, and **2 in practice** | The assessor **on that claim** (§4.1) | With one approver who never assesses, one is enough. **The moment an approver also assesses — likely on a small team — every claim they touched needs a second approver**, and there is not one |
| `ADMIN` | **2** | Each other, on the threshold change (§4.7) and the import commit (§4.9) | **Both rules are already live** (§0.2's note). One administrator means the auto-approval threshold cannot be changed at all and a group load cannot be committed |
| `TERM_AUTHOR` | **1** | — | One author is workable. The register is ten terms and one product |
| `TERM_APPROVE` | **2** | The author **of that version** (§4.25) | **This is the binding constraint and it is A3-10's question.** With one holder who also authors, every term they write is unapprovable and the register stalls; with one holder who authors nothing, the register depends on one person's availability |
| **The checkpoint queue** (§4.26) | **1**, and it must be somebody's job | — | **Nobody is assigned to it in any document** — D1 v0.5 `B-07`. It is not a new grant: any `ASSESSOR` holds it. **What is missing is an owner**, and a queue with no owner is a queue with an ageing clock and no reader |
| **A person who can correct a renewal status in InsureMO** | **1**, if the answer to `C-IMO-15` is *yes* | — | Framework v2.5 opened **C-IMO-15** because §4.21's re-read exists to pick up a correction **nothing in MVP 1 can make**. **The arithmetic, stated because the point of the table is the number at the bottom.** Taking every *at least* above and allowing one person to hold several grants, the floor is **four people**, and it is four only if the two administrators are also the two term approvers and neither of them ever assesses a claim. **A pilot team of three cannot satisfy all three two-person rules**, whatever the allocation: two administrators plus a separate assessor and approver is already four, and collapsing the approver onto an administrator puts the person who sets the auto-approval threshold in the seat that approves the claims it lets through. **That is not a rule this matrix carries and it should be**, which is the second half of **A3-13**.

> **§4.1 is the one that will be argued about.** In a pilot team of three people, requiring a different person to approve looks like friction. It is the only control on the amount paid, and every claim system that removed it regretted it. The correct relief valve is the auto-approval threshold (§4.7) — small claims flow without a human at all — not weakening segregation on the ones large enough to need review.

## 5. Audit — who may read what

Audit logging (F63) records every decision and data edit as JSON. Reading it is itself a permission.

| Reader | May read |
|---|---|
| `ASSESSOR`, `APPROVER` | The processing trail of claims in their scope: state changes, decisions, corrections, who and when |
| `ASSESSOR`, `APPROVER`, `ADMIN` | **Their own staff notifications, and only their own** (A2 §8.1). New at v1.2. A notification is addressed to a user; **no role reads another user's inbox and there is no supervisor digest** — that would be the cross-user report on the row below, arriving through a side door |
| Anyone who may open the claim | **The claim's correspondence history** — every message sent about it, its code, its template version, its address and its delivery outcome. New at v1.2. **Wider than the outbox it supplements** (F61's outbox is an administrator's screen showing failures only), because the person a customer telephones is the assessor holding the claim, and until now they had nowhere to look. **`ADMIN` sees the rows in full with bodies quoting clinical detail withheld under a visible note** — §4.11, never silently absent |
| `ADMIN` | Configuration and access events across the system — **not** claim clinical detail |
| Nobody, in MVP 1 | A cross-user report of "what did person X do" — that is an audit function, and no audit role exists yet |

**Two properties, and both are contractual.** The audit log is **append-only** — no role, including `ADMIN`, may edit or delete an entry. And **reading a claim's clinical detail is itself logged**, so who looked at whose medical documents is answerable. The second is cheap to build now and effectively impossible to reconstruct later.

**Deliberately deferred:** a dedicated `AUDITOR` role with read-across-everything. It belongs with the multi-level authority matrix deferred by declared simplification S-06, and creating it without those surrounding controls would produce an account with universal read access and no oversight.

## 6. Enforcement — where the control actually lives

| Layer | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| **API** | **The control.** Every endpoint checks role *and* data scope on every call, from the token, ignoring anything the client asserts |
| **UI** | Convenience. Hides what the user may not do; never the enforcement point |
| **Data scope** | Applied as a **predicate at the query layer**, not as a filter after fetching. A record outside scope is never loaded, so it cannot leak through an error message, a count, or a log line |
| **Audit** | Every denied attempt is logged with role, scope, resource and correlation id |

**Denials are logged, not just refused.** A pattern of denied attempts is the earliest signal of either a misconfigured role or a user probing, and neither is visible if refusals are silent.

## 8. Change control

A permission changes here before it changes in code. Adding a **—** is additive. Changing a **—** to **F**, or relaxing any rule in §4, is a change request against the Framework and is recorded with the reason — because every one of those rules exists to prevent a specific failure, and the reason must survive the person who wrote it.

**From v1.5, a third addition, and it is the shortest rule in this document.** **A grant carries the scope its data actually has, and a screen may not render data from a wider scope than the grant it is behind.** §4.29 is the first application: a `MASTER`-scoped grant renders counts and dates and never a claim number, **whatever second grant the acting user happens to hold**. The reason it belongs in change control is that the pressure to break it arrives as a usability request — *the approver cannot see which claims* — and it is a reasonable request with a wrong answer. **The right answer to it is a different grant, not a wider screen**, and the moment that trade is made through a control on a screen rather than through a scope at the query layer, §0's second question stops being answerable.

**From v1.4, a second addition, and it is narrower than it looks.** **A new grant over contractual text arrives with a four-eyes rule or it does not arrive.** §4.25 is the first; there is no second yet. The reason it is written into change control rather than left as a §4 rule is that **the term register is the first thing in this system a user can author that binds a customer**, and the next such thing — a wording store entry, a settlement-letter clause, a decline-reason extension — will be added by somebody reading §4.24 and copying it. **The four-eyes half is the part that will be dropped as friction**, because it is the part that costs a second person, and §4.1 exists in this document precisely because that argument has been made before and lost.

**From v1.2, one addition to that.** **A new configuration value that the calculation chain reads arrives with an effective-dating rule or it does not arrive.** §4.7a states the property; the reason it is written into change control rather than left as a §4 rule is that §4.7 went eighteen days without one and nobody noticed, because the rule that should have caught it was written about a different field. **The next such value will be added by somebody reading §4.14 or §4.7 and copying it — so both now say the same thing, and §4.7a says why.**
